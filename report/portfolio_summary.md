# Aboveground Carbon Recovery After Swidden Agriculture
## Portfolio Summary

**Author:** Mohamed Timothy Kabba
**Degree:** MSc Forestry (Forest Biometrics and Remote Sensing), Njala University
**Location:** Bojene and Kasewe Hills Forest Reserves, Sierra Leone

---

### Background

Swidden agriculture (slash and burn farming) is the dominant land use system across upland Sierra Leone. When farmers abandon a plot, the forest begins to regenerate through a predictable sequence of growth stages. Understanding how fast carbon stocks recover is essential for forest management policy, REDD+ carbon accounting, and decisions about minimum fallow periods.

### Objectives

1. Establish an old-growth carbon baseline from mature forest within Kasewe Hills Forest Reserve.
2. Quantify aboveground carbon stocks across four fallow age classes.
3. Model the rate of carbon recovery with increasing fallow age.
4. Test local measurements against IPCC Tier 1 default values.

### Methods

Seventy plots (40 x 40 m) were established: 10 permanent control plots inside Kasewe Hills Forest Reserve and 60 temporary fallow plots across 20 surrounding communities (10 around Kasewe in Moyamba District, 10 around Bojene in Bo District). Each community contributed one farmbush, one young secondary, and one old secondary plot. Every stem with DBH >= 10 cm was recorded. A sub-sample of 359 trees was measured for height, and a Michaelis-Menten height-diameter model was fitted to predict missing heights. Aboveground biomass was estimated using the Chave et al. (2014) pantropical equation with wood density values from the Global Wood Density Database (prioritising West African regional values). Carbon stock was calculated as AGB x 0.47 (IPCC default).

### Results

| Age Class | Mean AGC (Mg C ha-1) | % of Control |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------|
| Farmbush | 1.02 | 0.7% |
| Young Secondary | 6.39 | 4.7% |
| Old Secondary | 18.84 | 13.8% |
| Control | 136.70 | 100% |

AGC differed significantly among age classes (ANOVA: F(3, 59) = 151.5, P < 0.001), and all pairwise comparisons were significant (Tukey HSD, P < 0.05). The Kruskal-Wallis test confirmed the result non-parametrically (H = 36.49, P < 0.001). Carbon recovery proceeded at approximately 2.41 Mg C ha-1 yr-1, with an estimated 60 years needed to reach old-growth parity. Basal area explained 95.9% of the variation in AGC (R-squared = 0.959), confirming it as a reliable and cost-effective field proxy.

### Limitations

The chronosequence approach substitutes space for time and assumes plots of similar age share comparable site histories. Natural variation in soils, slope, and land use intensity introduces uncertainty. There is no old-growth baseline inside Bojene, so the Kasewe control serves both sites. Results apply to the moist forest zone of southern Sierra Leone and may not transfer directly to other regions.

### Recommendations

1. Fallow periods of at least 15 to 20 years are needed before meaningful carbon accumulation occurs; shorter rotations keep forests in a permanently low-carbon state.
2. Basal area can serve as a rapid field indicator for carbon monitoring where full allometric data collection is impractical.
3. Protecting mature forest remnants is far more efficient for carbon retention than relying on secondary forest regrowth alone.
